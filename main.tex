\lettrineabstract{Healthcare is changing rapidly: technology is evolving, and new treatments are being implemented at a breakneck pace on a global scale.
However, healthcare still remains limited in many aspects. One critical aspect is health and wellbeing for the elderly population.
While technology enabling effective care for vulnerable populations has increased, many needs remain unmet. Among them are access to healthcare,
effective nutrition and food technology, availability of tools for medication management and rehabilitation, support for mental health,
as well as community-driven care. This booklet aims to explore these topics briefly and provide a foundation to solve issues at large.}

\section{Ageing Societies}
In the 21st century, both developed and developing countries are rapidly ageing. The percentage of citizens aged over 60 (defined as ``elderly citizens'' \cite{reporttcdc}) is rising in almost all developed countries worldwide. This demographic trend poses a challenge
for a wide multitude of sectors, including finance, entertainment, and healthcare. Thailand is no exception to the ageing society trend,
as it is rapidly transitioning into a super-aged society, with 28\% of its population expected to be aged 60 or older by 2030. This shift
is transforming every aspect of Thai society---from healthcare and financial systems to economic productivity and family structures.

This transition does not occur in a vacuum: aspects interacts with each other and often results in great additive effects.
One commonly cited example in economics is the indirect effect of an ageing population on the labour supply, which is best explained by
the requirements imposed resulting in a restructuring of roles within the family unit (where working-aged adults are moved away
from the labour force towards caregiving). While not directly relevant to the present context of this hackathon, such effects do not
limit their influence to merely the economic sector and labour force. Healthcare as a sector is also heavily affected as well. The
direct and indirect effects of healthcare from these factors is left as an exercise to the reader.

Thailand's healthcare system is overall considered one of the best globally, with a rank of 5 out of 195 countries according to \citet{ThailandGHSIndex}.
This composite ranking considers a holistic perspective on the healthcare system as a whole, including elements such as the accessibility of
healthcare, the availability of prehospital care, disease response capability and detection capacity among other metrics. However, these metrics
paint a different story than the picture that most Thais (and HCWs) see of the healthcare system on a daily basis, with overcrowding,
long wait times and, in distant districts, a distinct lack of HCWs.

\subsection{Inequity of Healthcare}
The availability of healthcare within countries is not distributed evenly. The highest proportion of healthcare workers (HCWs) tend to
be found in the most developed areas. This is especially true for Thailand, where the ``primate city'' of Bangkok serves as a hub
for a large number of world-class healthcare facilities, while remote provinces often receive care that may be considered as below the
standard set by big cities. In fact, over four million people in Thailand alone are without ready access to healthcare services
\cite{tilakulThailandsHealthSystem2026}. Factors driving this inequity to healthcare include the lack of homogeneity in development,
the selection pool for training of HCWs, uneven distribution of funding and human factors among others. While initiatives such as
those by the NHSO exist to allow patients access to hospital, they are often found to have limited scope in practice, resulting
in ineffective solutions to the problem of healthcare access. This is shown best in \ref{medrole_fig}, which shows how doctors are
distributed unevenly to the patient population, and that workload differs greatly across healthcare districts. One potential
solution involves increasing funding and availability of healthcare centers in distant provinces, however, the feasibility of
such solutions often depend on individual community context. Consequently, a comprehensive solution remains notably absent.

\subsection{Funding Public Projects}
As the population ages, the proportion of the working class continually decreases, resulting in a decreased amount of
revenue collected in taxes. While taxes are always controversial, such decreases as a result of a shrinking worker
demographic undeniably results in less funds spent in the development of infrastructure and other essential public
utilities. These factors could increase the prevalence of preventable diseases such as water–bourne agents, mosquito
populations and other sanitation based maladies, leading an already overloaded healthcare system at the brink of collapse.
Furthermore, social welfare systems, including in Thailand, are at great risk as goverments rush to cut spending and
adapt to the challenge presented. More information on this topic may be found here: \citet{ThailandsAgeingSociety2025},
as well as in socioeconomics and the ``dependancy ratio.''

With these considerations, many policy projects focus on how to enable governments to function in this state.
However, the details of such projects and their context is beyond the context of this article. However, meaningful and
cost–conscious solutions to this slowly developing problem are sorely lacking, especially in the healthcare sector and
healthcare services delivery.



\section{Social Context}
Healthcare is more than just treating symptoms and prescribing medicines for illness. Effective healthcare demands
the involvement of HCWs in the community, both at the individual and community scale. Consequently,
community relationships serve as a key method for the prevention of disease and to increase the availability of healthcare
within communities, especially those in remote areas. Thailand is unique in this context, with a large network of community
HCWs known as ``village health volunteers (VHVs)'' who serve to protect the health of their communities, monitor
vulnerable residents and effectively report community health status to the relevant authorities to promote informed policy
decisions that reflect community status at both the community and country scale by narrowing the gap between policymakers
and residents of the local area. (One way to define the decentralization of healthcare is the term ``community based care'')

Community based care is an effective system, though it has some limitations, including the risk of decreasing equity
of care, to the limited technology available for VHVs. Solutions to these problems are few and far between, but
could hold the potential to be highly effective and beneficial for the community at large.
\subsection{Expectations of Healthcare}
Thailand healthcare system is accessible to many, which is often touted as a great success of the design. However, this is
accompanied by large patient loads, primarily as a result of ease of access. According to \citet{MOHreport}, the number
of outpatient visits per hundred thousand population hovers around 30,000 in every year, indicating significant resource
utilization and benefit to the population. However, ease of access also gives rise to outpatient visits that may not be
deemed strictly necessary. While Thailand is aiming to cut these visits by public initiatives such as incorporating
pharmacy prescriptions into the \emph{gold card} program, allowing for basic prescriptions to be dispensed without the need
to visit the hospital. Considering the current situation, more and timely solutions are required to maintain the quality of
essential hospital functions, which the government is processing. Some solutions that have not yet been implemented in
full scale projects include telehealth solutions, which have origins from the \textit{Coronavirus} pandemic.
However, private sector solutions may also be considered for implementation in the present context.

\subsection{The Mental Health Stigma}
As a society, Thailand is developed in some aspects, and below international standards on others. One major factor
where Thailand requires improvement is the mental health stigma: the majority of Thais harbour negative opinions towards
receiving mental health treatment for fear of a social stigma. While social change is slow, it is direly needed to ensure
that the population is able and \emph{especially willing} to receive treatment, especially as the country ages and the
ropes of the loneliness epidemic tighten day–by–day.

\subsection{Healthy Habits}
One challenge to providing effective preventative healthcare for the general population is the lack of
action regarding healthy eating habits. The typical Thai meal does not cover all nutritional bases, and often
exceeds healthy levels for many macronutrients, including calories, carbohydrates, fats, salt and sugar. The habit of choosing
food that is highly flavorful and relatively in excess compared to the nutritional requirements may result in long-term
consequences such as the increased risk for NCDs, including renal, cardiovascular and diabetes related complications.

\subsection{Life Quality \& Interventions}
The quality of life of a population is determined by a wide range of factors, including social environment,
region, social equity and personal factors among others. Though over 30 years has passed, the Dahlgren and Whitehead model
remains one of the standard ways of understanding the influences of health even before a visit to the medical practitioner.
Many solutions for wellbeing do not start in the hospital, but instead in the community, where interventions are often more
effective than treatment of already developed conditions. The prevention of disease, especially non-communicable diseases
remains more cost effective than treatment. However, many limitations remain that result in social interventions being overlooked.
Solutions in this field are direly needed, and may serve to actively enable the prevention of diseases in the subclinical phase,
resulting in healthier communities and longer healthspans for the population.

Any effective interventions on life quality for the population, especially in the elderly population, must take into account
the feasibility of these interventions in actual practice. Many a lesson has been learned by well-meaning NGOs and even
governmental aid organizations that intervention effectiveness is not determined by the quality of each product or commercial,
but the feasibility of adoption to daily life is the factor which indicates if interventions will prove successful in the long run.
One example of this is the well-meaning diabetes diet and exercise regimens, which often face failure as a consequence of how
patients are not able to adhere to them, and often end up reverting to their original, disease causing, diets. Therefore,
one could speculate that more realistic interventions based on individual ability could drive better compliance and
greater success \cite{samiEffectDietType2017}. Put simply, behavioral friction kills effective interventions and
technology. For further details on policy interventions, please see \citet{weaverWillItWork}.

\section{Emerging Technologies}
Overall, artificial intelligence (AI) is emerging as a powerful tool for change in healthcare systems and technology.
From AI text transformers to advanced data processing and analytics, AI holds the potential to improve access to healthcare
and increase the efficiency of Thailand’s medical infrastructure. However, several barriers still hinder the widespread adoption
of AI in healthcare. These include technological complexity, lack of cultural sensitivity, and a failure to address the specific
needs of the Thai elderly population and their families.
